Basketball Shot Tracker

Arthur De Souza and Shravya Sivanathan

Fall 2026 ECE 4554 Computer Vision: Project Proposal

Virginia Tech

Problem Statement

When a shooter shoots the basketball, a make or miss does not define if the shooter had good form or poor form. This project aims to develop a basketball shot tracker that analyzes a shooter's technique independent of whether it goes in the hoop or not. This will be done by analyzing data gathered from public sources as well as videos recorded by the team.

The proposed system will estimate the shooter's body pose, assess shooting form, and automatically determine whether the shot was made or missed. It will analyze these factors and assign each shot to one of the following four categories:

Proposed shot categories
Category Form assessment Shot outcome
Good make Good form Made shot
Bad make Poor form Made shot
Good miss Good form Missed shot
Bad miss Poor form Missed shot

"Good form" and "poor form" refer to criteria defined by the team prior to labeling the dataset. The criteria will be based on reference footage, possibly including shots by Klay Thompson. The assessment should take into account that different shooters have different styles, instead of treating one player's shot as the only correct way to shoot. If possible, the system will also give feedback on specific parts of the shooter's form.

Planned Approach

The form analysis pipeline will utilize a similar technique developed by Arthur when he worked on a hand sign recognition project, which includes extracting landmark coordinates, normalizing the data, and classifying using a random forest classifier. In this case, the body pose estimator will detect landmarks like shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, and knees from each video frame. As of right now, MediaPipe Pose Landmarker will be used [1].

Normalization will involve scaling landmark coordinates according to the size of the shooter's hips and torso length in order to remove the impact of body size and placement in the picture. Features, like elbow joint angles, degree of bending in the knee joint, and shooting wrist height above the shoulders, will be gathered based on landmark coordinates.

Since jump shooting is a motion and not just a stationary pose, some features will be extracted from certain parts in the jump shot's motion. The setup phase will be determined as the frame with the maximum knee bend before the jump, while the release phase will be the frame with maximum shooting wrist height.

The random forest classifier will be trained on how to recognize good and bad form based on these features with help of the scikit-learn library [2]. For the baseline approach, a rule-based evaluation based on comparing those features with explicit thresholds (i.e. elbow angle at release time) will be considered.

Since body form is not enough to determine if the basket was scored, the outcome of the shot will be determined using a pretrained object detector: Ultralytics YOLO [3]. YOLO will detect the ball in the frame, which, if necessary, can be trained on a small amount of labeled frames. In the case of a stationary camera, the position of the basket in the frame can be determined once per clip. This can be done by a detector or by manually marking the rim area. If the center of the ball enters the rim from the top and goes out the bottom, the shot will be determined as "made". The OpenCV library [4] will be used for video processing and tracking.

There are some issues, however, as the viewpoint of the camera can affect pose measurements, and the ball and hoop must be visible for outcome detection. Therefore, the initial scope is limited to a stationary camera, one shooter, and side or angled views where the shooter, ball, and hoop are all in frame.

Plans for Experiments

Datasets

The dataset will consist of a combination of two datasets: publicly available clips from the internet (i.e. shooting tutorials and practice shots) and clips recorded by the team that clearly show the shooter, ball, and hoop. Using both datasets, the team aims to obtain around 200 jump shot attempts, with approximately an equal amount of makes and misses.

Data Collection Procedure

The team will record shots at a basketball court using a phone. The camera will be placed so that it shows the shooter, ball, and hoop fully in the frame. The shot outcome will be recorded and utilized as ground truth. Afterward, both team members will categorize each shot's form based on the criteria.

Code: Written vs. Borrowed

Borrowed (existing libraries and models) Implemented by the team
MediaPipe Pose Landmarker for pose estimation; scikit-learn random forest; pretrained YOLO object detector; OpenCV for video I/O Landmark normalization and feature extraction; shot-stage detection; rule-based baseline; ball-trajectory and make/miss logic; combination into four categories; labeling and evaluation scripts

Experiments

  1. Compare the performance of the random forest against the rule based baseline on unseen shots, measuring accuracy and F1 score. The test set will include shots made by shooters not included in the training set.
  2. Use the random forest's feature importance scores to determine which measurements (i.e. elbow angle at ball release) have the most weight when it comes to deciding good form. Also train separate models using both setup and release features, as well as models for each stage alone, to determine which stage of the shot provides the most data.
  3. Compare make/miss results produced by the system against the actual recorded outcomes to measure accuracy. The incorrect predictions will be reviewed to determine the cause (i.e. player blocking rim).
  4. Measure accuracy of the final output, where each shot is placed into one of the four categories.
  5. Compare the system's performance using different camera angles, along with the dataset footage, to determine how much video angle and quality affect results.

Definition of Success

The project will be a success if the system has around 80% categorization accuracy on clips of shooters it was not previously trained on. Not only that, make/miss detection should have around 90% accuracy on valid clips. The final product should be a working system that takes in a video, places each shot into one of the categories, and possibly provides feedback on the shot.

Expected Findings and Uncertainties

The team expects that elbow and wrist measurements at the release of the ball will have the most weight when determining the form category. Make/miss detection is also expected to work well from a side view, where the path of the ball is easy to see. However, there are a few uncertainties. Pose estimation may be less accurate at the release, when the shooter's arms can block their head. In addition, around 200 shots may not be enough for the system to work well on new shooters. Lastly, the public clips may not have a standard camera angle or video quality, especially in comparison to the team's clips, which can lower the accuracy.
